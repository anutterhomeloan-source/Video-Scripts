## Hook

If you're under contract and you haven't locked your rate yet, this week should have scared you.
If you don't know who I am, my name is Jordan Nutter, and I help make mortgages make sense.

## Problem

Freddie Mac's weekly average jumped a quarter point in one week.
The biggest jump in four years.
And if you were floating, meaning you hadn't locked yet, you felt that.

## Empathy

And honestly? Nobody explains this part.
You get the house under contract, and everyone's talking about inspections and appraisals.
Nobody says, "Hey. When are we locking?"

## Answer

So here's what a rate lock is.
It's your lender guaranteeing your rate for a set amount of time while your loan closes.
Usually 30, 45, or 60 days.
If rates go up during that window, yours doesn't.
Here's what that's worth.
Say you're borrowing $400,000.
At 7%, your principal and interest is about $2,661 a month.
At 7.5%, it's about $2,797.
That's $136 a month.
Over 30 years? Almost $49,000.
VISUAL: Two-row table: 7.0% → $2,661/mo · 7.5% → $2,797/mo · "+$136/mo · ~$49,000 over 30 yrs"

But here's what a lock doesn't protect.
If your lock runs out before you close, extending it can cost money.
And if something in your loan changes, like your credit, your down payment, or your loan amount, your rate can change too.
A lock protects you from the market.
Not from changes in your file.

## Change

So ask your lender three things.
One. When are we locking?
Two. How long is the lock, and does it cover my closing date?
Three. If rates drop after I lock, do you offer a float-down?
Some lenders do. Some don't. Just ask.

That's okay. Just know you're making it.
