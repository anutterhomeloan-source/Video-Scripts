## Hook

Most people I talk to right now can afford the house.
That's not what's stopping them.
And if that's you, you're not behind.
You're being careful.
If you don't know who I am, my name is Jordan Nutter, and I help make mortgages make sense.

## Problem

You bought a few years ago.
You've got a rate with a 2 or a 3 in front of it.
And now the house is too small.
But moving means giving that rate up for one with a 6 or a 7.
VISUAL: "2.75%" crossed out, arrow to "7%"

## Empathy

Nobody prepares you for this part.
And honestly? You did everything right.
You bought. You built equity. You kept your payment low.
So of course you don't want to blow it.

## Answer

Here's what I think is really going on.
You know when you book family flights for three thousand dollars…
and a week later, the same flights are eighteen hundred?
You could afford the three thousand.
That's not why it stings.
It stings because you feel dumb.
VISUAL: Text call-out "$3,000 → $1,800" with a facepalm reaction GIF
That's what this decision feels like.
Except it's a house.
And there's no refund.

## Change

So I'm not going to try to convince you rates will drop.
I'm going to show you the worst case.
What if you buy, and have to sell in three years?
What if rates never come down?
What if they do?
When you see the real numbers, the worst case is usually way smaller than the fear.
VISUAL: Three-line checklist: "Sell in 3 years? / Rates stay put? / Rates drop?"

## Stance

Here's my take.
The rate isn't the scary part.
Not knowing your worst case is.

## Caption (approved)
If you can afford the move but can't make yourself do it, the rate usually isn't the real problem. It's the fear of making a call you can't undo. The fix isn't guessing where rates go. It's looking at the worst case with real numbers: what if you sell in three years, what if rates stay put, what if they drop. Most people find the worst case is something they can live with.
